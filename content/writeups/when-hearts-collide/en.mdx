## **TryHackMe: When Hearts Collide — Breaking a Photo Matchmaker with an MD5 Collision**

_A walkthrough of the "When Hearts Collide" challenge from TryHackMe's Love at First Breach 2026 event. The site is a dog-photo "Matchmaker" that decides whether two pictures are the same by comparing their MD5 hashes. MD5 has been collision-broken since 2004, so we generate two visibly different JPEGs that share one MD5 with fastcoll, feed them to the matcher, and it happily declares a "match" — handing over the flag._

> The flag and the target IP are redacted. When Hearts Collide is an authorized training challenge on TryHackMe; everything here was performed against a legal practice target. The flag is shown as `THM{<redacted>}`; the real value was recovered on the box but is withheld here.

---

### **TL;DR — the whole chain**

<Callout type="tldr">

The Matchmaker identifies and compares photos by their **MD5 hash** → MD5 is **collision-broken** (Wang et al., 2004), and the app even leaks the hint by displaying the sample dog's MD5 `79054025255fb1a26e4bc422aef54eb4` — the exact digest from the famous 2004 collision → we run **fastcoll** to produce two visibly different images (`twin1.jpg`, `twin2.jpg`) with an **identical MD5** → upload them to the Matchmaker → it compares MD5s, sees them equal, and declares **"Match complete"** at `/upload_success/<uuid>` → the match page reveals the **flag**.

Different pictures, one hash, an instant (bogus) match.

</Callout>

### **The target**

```text
export IP=<target-ip>
```

When Hearts Collide is a dog-photo "Matchmaker" web app served on port 80:

```text
http://$IP/             →  Matchmaker homepage (upload a photo to "match")
http://$IP/view/<uuid>  →  a stored match, serving /static/uploads/<uuid>.jpg
```

The homepage links a sample match whose photo is served from `/static/uploads/00795a8b-….jpg`, and the app prints that photo's MD5:

```text
79054025255fb1a26e4bc422aef54eb4
```

That value is not random. It's the MD5 from **Xiaoyun Wang et al.'s 2004 collision** — the first public proof that MD5 is broken. A known collision hash printed right on the page is a loud hint: **the matching logic trusts MD5.**

### **The vulnerability — MD5 used as an identity check**

The Matchmaker decides whether two photos "match" by comparing their **MD5 digests**. MD5 was proven collision-vulnerable in 2004, when Wang et al. produced two different 128-byte messages with the _same_ MD5 hash — meaning MD5 can no longer reliably tell two files apart.

<Callout type="insight">

Because the app treats **"same MD5" as "same / matching image"**, anyone who can supply two files that collide can make the app call two _different_ pictures a match. And generating identical-prefix MD5 collisions on commodity hardware takes seconds with public tooling — so this is trivial to weaponize, not a theoretical worry.

</Callout>

### **Step 1 — build the collision tool (fastcoll)**

```text
sudo apt install build-essential libboost-all-dev git
git clone https://github.com/upbit/clone-fastcoll.git
cd clone-fastcoll
make
```

### **Step 2 — generate two images with the same MD5**

Point fastcoll at a seed image; it emits two outputs that differ in a few bytes but share one MD5:

```text
./fastcoll dog.jpg
# Generating first block: ...
# Generating second block: ...
# use 'md5sum md5_data*' check MD5

md5sum md5_data*
# 7f493964558792b6931345242fd1718d  md5_data1
# 7f493964558792b6931345242fd1718d  md5_data2   <- identical MD5, different files
```

Rename the two outputs to valid-looking image uploads:

```text
mv md5_data1 twin1.jpg
mv md5_data2 twin2.jpg
```

We now hold two JPEGs that are byte-for-byte different yet hash to the **same** MD5.

### **Step 3 — feed the twins to the Matchmaker**

Upload the two colliding images through the Matchmaker's upload form. The app hashes each file, compares the digests, finds them **equal**, and declares a match — exactly the integrity decision MD5 can no longer be trusted to make. The result lands on a match page:

```text
http://$IP/upload_success/<uuid>
# "Match complete"  —  YOUR UPLOAD  <->  DOGGY DATABASE
```

Two different dog photos, declared a match on hash equality alone. The match page prints the flag:

```text
THM{<redacted>}
```

Different images → one MD5 → bogus "match" → flag. The entire trick rests on the app trusting a hash that stopped being collision-resistant two decades ago.

### **Impact**

<Callout type="critical">

**Medium — CVSS 3.1 5.3** (`AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:N/I:H/A:N`). In this challenge the payoff is just the flag, but the underlying flaw — **using MD5 to decide whether two files are the same** — is a genuine integrity failure:

- **Integrity bypass.** An attacker who controls the inputs can make two _different_ files pass as identical. Wherever MD5 gates trust — deduplication, "known-good" allow-lists, checksum/signature verification, content-addressed storage — a benign file and a malicious one can be swapped under one hash.
- **Cheap and deterministic.** Identical-prefix MD5 collisions take seconds with public tools; chosen-prefix collisions (as weaponized against MD5 in the Flame malware, and against SHA-1 in "SHAttered") extend the same idea to realistic, attacker-chosen file contents.

</Callout>

### **Recommended fix — stop trusting MD5**

<Callout type="fix">

1. **Replace MD5 for any integrity or identity decision** with a collision-resistant hash — **SHA-256** or stronger. MD5 (and SHA-1) must not be used to decide whether two files are "the same" or "known good."
2. **Don't equate "same hash" with "same content"** for security decisions. If you must detect duplicates, compare the bytes once hashes match, and use authenticated, keyed constructions (HMAC) where a secret is involved.
3. **For signatures / allow-listing**, verify with a modern signature scheme over a strong hash, never a bare digest comparison.

</Callout>

### **Takeaways**

- **MD5 is not an identity.** "Equal MD5" has meant "possibly a crafted collision" since 2004 — it is no proof that two files are the same.
- **Read the hints the app leaks.** A printed hash of `79054025255fb1a26e4bc422aef54eb4` _is_ the exploit brief: it's the canonical Wang collision digest.
- **Collisions are a commodity.** `fastcoll` turns the theory into two files in seconds — the attack is practical, not academic.
- **Use SHA-256 for integrity.** The whole bug class disappears when matching and verification use a collision-resistant hash.

Thanks for reading. "When Hearts Collide" is a tidy reminder that MD5 collisions aren't a museum piece — anywhere a system decides "same file?" by comparing MD5, two hearts (and two very different files) can be made to collide.
